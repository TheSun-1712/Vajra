# Problem Statement (as given)

Turbulence in the atmosphere distorts a plane-parallel wavefront propagating through it. A Shack-Hartmann Wavefront Sensor (SH-WFS) samples this distorted wavefront using an array of small lenslets (the Microlens Array/MLA), which creates a spot-field on the detector. The deviation of these spots from their reference positions is used to derive the reconstructed wavefront and its associated Zernike coefficients. The conjugate of this reconstructed wavefront is used to derive an actuator command map, in units of actuator stroke length, which is fed to a Deformable Mirror (DM) to correct the distortion in real time.

This pipeline — **spot detection → centroid/deviation measurement → wavefront/Zernike reconstruction → conjugation → DM command → correction** — is the backbone of every SH-WFS AO system, including what NLST will need. Each stage of this pipeline has specific, well-documented failure modes. SURYA addresses every stage.

Below, problems are organised by **which stage of the pipeline** they break, then solved with technical specificity at the level of what's actually measured, computed, and commanded.

---

## STAGE 1: Spot Detection on the Detector

### Problem 1 — Spot detection on an extended source (the Sun)
**Mechanism:** The reference-position deviation measurement assumes each lenslet's PSF is a clean, identifiable spot. For a point star this is just a diffraction-limited blob — centroiding is trivial (intensity-weighted center of mass). For the Sun, each lenslet subaperture instead images a patch of **solar granulation** — a textured, evolving scene with no single peak. There is no "spot" to centroid in the classical sense.

**Why existing fixes fall short:** Correlation-based solar SH-WFS cross-correlates the current subimage against a stored reference granulation image to find the shift. This works only while the granulation pattern resembles the reference — but granules evolve (5–10 min lifetime), and under strong seeing the subimage is smeared enough that the correlation peak becomes broad/ambiguous, especially for the higher spatial-frequency content that carries high-order Zernike information.

**Solution — Reference-free extended-scene sensing (Layer 0):**
A CNN with spatial attention ingests the *entire* raw MLA detector frame (all subimages jointly, not one at a time) and regresses Zernike coefficients directly — skipping the centroid/cross-correlation step entirely. Training pairs are generated by forward-modelling: take real solar granulation images (e.g. from public SST/DKIST archives), apply simulated phase screens (HCIPy, Hanle Cn² profile) to generate the corresponding distorted Hartmannogram, and use the known input phase as ground truth. The network learns the statistical fingerprint of *turbulence-induced* deformation (local shift + local blur + spot elongation) as separable from *granulation* structure, because turbulence has known spatial-correlation properties (Kolmogorov statistics) that real granulation does not share. Joint processing of all subapertures lets the network use cross-subaperture consistency as an implicit prior, the way classical reconstruction uses the geometry matrix — but learned rather than hand-designed.

---

### Problem 2 — Spot detection under low photon flux
**Mechanism:** Centroid noise scales inversely with the number of detected photons in a subaperture: σ_centroid ∝ FWHM/SNR. For faint guide sources, partial cloud, or simply the lower-flux limbs/penumbrae of solar features, individual subaperture SNR drops and the measured spot position carries large random error that propagates straight into the reconstructed Zernike coefficients via the reconstruction matrix.

**Solution — Photon-statistics-aware confidence weighting (Layer 1a):**
Rather than centroiding every subaperture with equal trust, compute a per-subaperture confidence score from (a) the actual photon count in that subaperture this frame, (b) the measured spot FWHM (broader spot = more smeared = less reliable even at fixed flux), and (c) deviation from the locally-fit wavefront gradient (an outlier relative to its 8 neighbours is probably noise, not signal). Reconstruction becomes a confidence-weighted least squares fit (rather than a uniform least-squares fit across all subapertures), so low-SNR subapertures contribute less to the global Zernike solution instead of injecting noise equally with high-SNR ones. Train this end-to-end by injecting a physically realistic noise model (Poisson photon noise + detector read noise) at training time across a wide range of guide-source magnitudes, so the network learns the SNR-to-reliability mapping rather than having a fixed empirical noise floor coded in.

## STAGE 2: Deviation Measurement → Wavefront/Zernike Reconstruction

### Problem 3 — Centroid gain error / spot truncation
**Mechanism:** Under strong turbulence, spots can be displaced far enough to land in or near a neighbouring subaperture's box, or be elongated/split. The classical "deviation from reference position" measurement assumes a near-Gaussian unimodal spot within its assigned box — when this assumption breaks, the centroid is computed wrong (truncated centroid, cross-talk between adjacent lenslets), producing a *systematic* gain error (the measured slope underestimates the true slope) rather than just noise. This is a known but usually under-addressed limitation of classical SH-WFS — centroid gain compresses with increasing turbulence strength, exactly when accurate measurement is most needed.

**Solution — Dynamic-range-aware reconstruction:**
Fold the per-subaperture confidence map (Problem 2) with an explicit *turbulence-strength estimate* (computed from the previous frame's reconstructed r0/Strehl) to detect when a subaperture is likely in the truncation/cross-talk regime, and down-weight or flag it rather than silently trusting a biased measurement. Because Layer 0's CNN reconstructs directly from the full Hartmannogram image (not from a centroid-then-fit pipeline), it implicitly avoids the centroid-gain-error problem altogether — gain compression is a property of the *centroiding algorithm*, not of the raw image, so a network trained on the raw image under high-turbulence simulated data learns the correct (non-compressed) mapping directly. This is a concrete, named advantage of the reference-free approach over classical centroiding, worth stating explicitly in the proposal.

---

### Problem 4 — Zernike modal aliasing / fitting error
**Mechanism:** A finite MLA grid (N×N lenslets) can only sample and reconstruct a finite number of Zernike modes. Spatial frequencies in the true wavefront higher than what the lenslet sampling can resolve don't disappear — they alias into the lower-order modes you *are* trying to measure, corrupting them. This is a fundamental sampling-theorem limitation, not a noise problem, and gets worse as turbulence strength (and therefore the high-order content) increases relative to a fixed MLA pitch.

**Solution — Aliasing-aware mode weighting + matched MLA-sampling-rate Zernike truncation:**
Quantify, via the known D/r0 (aperture diameter over Fresnel scale) at Hanle and the chosen lenslet pitch, exactly how many Zernike modes can be reliably reconstructed before aliasing dominates (a standard but frequently skipped calculation — Noll/Fried sampling criteria). Explicitly truncate the reconstruction to that mode count rather than reconstructing more modes than the geometry supports and unknowingly aliasing noise into them. Combine this with a spatial-frequency-domain regularisation term in the network's loss function during training — penalising the network for assigning power to modes beyond the geometrically-supportable cutoff — so the learned reconstruction respects the sampling limit rather than confidently hallucinating high-order content from aliased input.

---

### Problem 5 — Non-uniform turbulence across the aperture (spatial gain)
**Mechanism:** Different regions of the aperture can be under different local turbulence strength (a cell of strong seeing over part of the pupil, calmer elsewhere). Classical modal gain optimisation tunes a single scalar gain per Zernike mode, applied identically everywhere in the aperture — it cannot represent "this mode is well-measured on the left side of the pupil and poorly measured on the right."

**Solution — 2D spatially-adaptive gain map (Layer 1b):**
Maintain a live gain value per subaperture (not per mode), updated every few hundred ms from the same confidence signals as Problem 2/3, and apply it directly in the slope-to-command path before modal decomposition. This is the spatial dimension that classical 1D modal gain optimisation cannot express. The two together — modal gain (temporal/per-mode) and spatial gain (per-subaperture) — form a genuinely 2D-adaptive gain architecture.

---

## STAGE 3: Reconstructed Wavefront → Conjugate → Actuator Map

### Problem 6 — Geometry mismatch between WFS sampling grid and DM actuator grid
**Mechanism:** The reconstructed wavefront/Zernike set lives on the WFS's lenslet sampling grid; the DM's actuator influence functions live on a different physical grid with their own coupling and edge effects. The standard "conjugate the wavefront, fit to actuator units of stroke" step relies on a fixed, pre-computed reconstruction-to-command matrix that assumes both grids and the actuator influence functions are static — but actuator influence functions are not perfectly known a priori and can change slightly with use (hysteresis, slight mechanical wear).

**Solution — Influence-function health check folded into Layer 5:**
Periodically (alongside the interaction-matrix health monitor), poke individual actuators with small known test signals during otherwise idle calibration windows and compare the measured WFS response to the assumed influence function. Drift in a specific actuator's influence function shows up as a localized discrepancy and triggers a targeted re-measurement of that actuator's row in the interaction/conjugation matrix — the same targeted-recalibration philosophy as the interaction matrix monitor, applied one level deeper to the influence-function assumption itself, which most systems never re-check at all once installed.

---

### Problem 7 — Actuator stroke saturation during conjugation
**Mechanism:** Converting the conjugate wavefront into actuator stroke units can demand more travel than a given actuator physically has, especially under strong turbulence concentrated in a local region of the pupil that maps to a few actuators. Naive handling either clips (leaves residual uncorrected error at exactly that location) or redistributes reactively only after the limit is hit (risking a saturation cascade to neighbours).

**Solution — Predictive saturation-aware command generation (Layer 4):**
Before the command is sent, a network trained offline to approximate a stroke-constrained least-squares solution (the mirror shape closest to the ideal conjugate that respects every actuator's stroke limit simultaneously) computes the safe command in microseconds — fast enough for kHz operation, where a true online QP solver would not be. The same module tracks each actuator's stroke usage trend over the last ~10 frames and begins gentle pre-emptive load-shifting to neighbours *before* the limit is reached, rather than only correcting after saturation occurs.

---

## STAGE 4: System-Level / Temporal Issues Around the Loop

### Problem 8 — Latency between measurement and actuation (the lag problem)
**Mechanism:** Spot detection, centroiding/reconstruction, conjugation, and DM actuation together take roughly 1–2ms. The correction applied at time t is therefore based on a wavefront measured at t−Δt — under frozen-flow assumptions this is fine if turbulence translates uniformly, but the moment wind shear, layer mixing, or a regime change occurs, the constant-velocity assumption underlying simple predictive control breaks.

**Solution — Regime-aware predictive control (Layer 2):**
Classify the turbulence regime (Steady / Transitioning / Broken) every few hundred ms using the *spatial correlation structure* of the measured slopes across the aperture — this changes shape before the temporal autocorrelation used by classical predictors starts failing, catching transitions earlier. Run three specialised predictors in parallel (aggressive linear/Transformer predictor for Steady, conservative short-horizon predictor for Transitioning, hold-and-decay stabiliser for Broken) and blend their outputs by the regime classifier's probabilities, avoiding discontinuous mirror commands at regime boundaries.

---

### Problem 9 — Drift of the interaction/reconstruction matrix over the observing run
**Mechanism:** The matrix mapping actuator pokes to slope measurements (and its inverse, used to go from slopes to commands) is calibrated once, but gravity-vector changes as NLST tracks the Sun across a wide elevation range through the day, plus thermal expansion, slowly invalidate it. By several hours in, commands computed from the stale matrix are systematically slightly wrong.

**Solution — Closed-loop residual health monitor (Layer 5):**
Continuously compute the cross-correlation between actuator command history and residual wavefront error history. A correctly-calibrated system shows small, random correlation; a drifted mode shows a specific, persistent actuator-residual correlation. Flag only the drifted modes and run a fast (~30s) targeted re-poke of just those modes instead of a full ~20-minute recalibration. Use telescope-tube temperature as a leading-indicator prior, since thermal drift direction is physically predictable, allowing pre-emptive recalibration timed to NLST's known elevation/temperature trajectory across the day.

---

### Problem 10 — Non-common-path aberrations (NCPA)
**Mechanism:** Any optical imperfection in the science-camera path that isn't shared with the WFS path (dust, coating non-uniformity, structural flexure downstream of the beamsplitter) is invisible to the wavefront sensor by construction — the SH-WFS only ever measures the path it samples. The correction loop happily reports a perfect Zernike fit while the science image still shows speckle from these uncorrected aberrations, and they drift slowly with temperature through the day.

**Solution — Speckle-plus-thermal NCPA tracker (Layer 6):**
A slow (~30–60s) background estimator infers the NCPA phase map from science-image speckle statistics and applies it as a DC bias on top of the main SH-WFS loop's DM command. Combine with telescope structural temperature sensors as a second, independent input: thermal expansion direction is physically predictable per structural element, so the thermal model anticipates which Zernike modes will drift before the speckle estimate confirms it — converging faster than either input alone.

---

### Problem 11 — Reduced field of view / isoplanatism beyond the guide point
**Mechanism:** The reconstructed wavefront and resulting actuator map are only strictly valid along the single line of sight sampled by the WFS. Atmosphere away from that line of sight (different cone through the turbulent layers) is under-corrected, and the well-corrected isoplanatic patch can be just a few arcseconds — small relative to a multi-arcminute solar active region.

**Solution — Ground-layer/high-altitude slope decomposition (Layer 3):**
Use the spatial coherence of slope measurements across the full aperture to separate the direction-independent ground-layer component (same correction works everywhere in the field) from the direction-dependent high-altitude component (only valid near the guide point), applying full correction gain to the former and reduced gain to the latter — extending the usable corrected field with the same single WFS/DM hardware, since ground layer is typically the dominant fraction of total turbulence.

---

### Problem 12 — Single-conjugate limit even after ground/high-altitude separation
**Mechanism:** A single DM, even with the gain split in Problem 11, can only physically hold one shape at a time — it cannot apply different high-altitude corrections to different parts of a wide field simultaneously, which is what true multi-conjugate AO (multiple DMs at different conjugate heights) would do.

**Solution — Temporal multiplexing across the camera exposure:**
Since the science camera integrates over many AO frames per readout, rapidly alternate the DM's target correction between two or three virtual conjugate states across successive control-loop frames within a single exposure, producing a science frame that is a time-average of multiple partially-corrected states rather than one narrowly-optimised state — a software approximation to multi-conjugate correction without additional DM hardware, at the cost of a small Strehl reduction at the original guide point.

---

### Problem 13 — Wavelength mismatch between sensing and science paths
**Mechanism:** The WFS commonly senses at a different wavelength band than the science camera observes (e.g., visible sensing, H-alpha or IR science). Because r0 scales as λ^6/5 and atmospheric dispersion is wavelength-dependent, a wavefront perfectly corrected at the sensing wavelength is subtly wrong at the science wavelength — a residual usually just absorbed as error rather than explicitly corrected.

**Solution — Chromatic offset model:**
Using the known λ^6/5 scaling law and Hanle's measured atmospheric dispersion across its operational zenith-angle range, compute a small predictive offset converting the WFS-wavelength correction into a science-wavelength correction, updated continuously as the Sun's zenith angle moves through its fully predictable daily path.

---

### Problem 14 — Mechanical vibration injected into the control loop
**Mechanism:** Cooling pumps, tracking motors, and wind buffeting inject narrowband mechanical vibration (commonly 10–100Hz) into tip-tilt and low-order modes that the SH-WFS measures and treats as if it were atmospheric. A wideband atmospheric controller is mistuned for a narrowband deterministic disturbance and can even amplify it through control-loop resonance if gain is pushed up generally to chase it.

**Solution — Adaptive notch filtering on the residual stream:**
Continuously FFT the tip-tilt/low-order residual time series; any sharp, time-stable peak distinct from the broadband Kolmogorov spectrum is flagged as mechanical and suppressed with an adaptive notch filter at exactly that frequency, with much higher gain than is safe for the broadband atmospheric loop (safe specifically because the disturbance is deterministic). The notch tracks automatically if pump cycling or wind conditions shift the frequency.

---

### Problem 15 — No uncertainty quantification anywhere in the reconstruction
**Mechanism:** Classical (and most neural) SH-WFS reconstructors output a single best-guess Zernike vector per frame with no associated confidence. The controller therefore cannot distinguish "this frame's correction is well-determined" from "this frame's correction is a guess under bad conditions" — it applies the same gain/aggressiveness regardless. RL-based AO controllers exist but are demonstrated only on pyramid WFS; Bayesian-network AO forecasting exists but only for predicting future DM voltages, not per-mode reconstruction uncertainty on an SH-WFS.

**Solution — Bayesian uncertainty-aware reconstruction and control (UARC layer):**
Replace the point-estimate reconstructor with a Bayesian neural network that outputs a calibrated variance per Zernike mode alongside the coefficient itself, trained specifically for the SH-WFS case (the unaddressed sensor type in existing uncertainty-aware AO work). Feed this per-mode uncertainty into the spatial/modal gain maps (Problems 5/2) and into the regime-aware predictor (Problem 8) — high-uncertainty modes get conservative gain and reduced predictive aggressiveness, closing the loop between "how sure are we" and "how hard do we correct," which classical reconstruction structurally cannot do.

---

### Problem 16 — No usable record of correction quality for science post-processing
**Mechanism:** After the loop runs, scientists analysing the corrected image need to know the actual PSF applied — but it changed every few seconds and varied across the field, and is essentially never independently measured during a solar observation.

**Solution — Telemetry-driven PSF reconstruction (Layer 7):**
Save compact per-frame state (residual Zernike vector, DM command, regime/uncertainty state) at a reduced rate sufficient for post-processing (not full-rate raw frames — addressing data volume directly), and fit a parametric PSF model per science exposure from this telemetry, accounting for residual atmospheric error, DM fitting error (Problem 4 truncation), temporal/prediction error (Problem 8), and NCPA (Problem 10) — producing a spatially varying PSF map scientists can use to deconvolve and recover resolution.

---

### Problem 17 — No way to validate any of this before NLST's AO hardware exists
**Mechanism:** Every solution above is justified by gaps in the literature, but none can be tested on real NLST sky data because the hardware doesn't exist yet — the most likely and most important question a review panel will ask.

**Solution — Three-stage validation roadmap:**
(1) Full simulation in HCIPy with Hanle-specific Cn² and wind statistics, covering the full pipeline end to end — start immediately. (2) Bench-top validation using a phase-plate/hot-air turbulence rig with a small real SH-WFS+DM, testing the sensing, reconstruction, and saturation-handling layers against genuine (if artificial) optical turbulence. (3) Validation of the extended-source (solar) sensing approach specifically against real granulation data via guest-observing time or data-sharing collaboration with an existing operational solar AO facility (DKIST, NVST), ahead of NLST's own AO commissioning.

## Problem 18 — Scintillation (amplitude errors mistaken for phase errors)
**Mechanism:** SH-WFS centroiding implicitly assumes each lenslet receives uniform, steady intensity, and that *only* the spot position carries information. But strong turbulence near the ground also causes scintillation — intensity fluctuations across the pupil, not just phase distortion. A subaperture experiencing a destructive-interference dip has a weak, noisy spot whose centroid is unreliable in a way that has nothing to do with its phase gradient — but the reconstructor has no way to know this is an intensity artefact rather than a real wavefront feature, especially for a daytime solar telescope where local near-ground turbulence (often strongest in solar, "seeing" dominated by ground-layer heating) makes scintillation worse than at a typical night site.

**Solution — Intensity-normalised confidence channel:**
Track per-subaperture total flux frame-to-frame as a separate signal from the centroid itself. A sudden, spatially localized flux dip with no corresponding change in neighboring subapertures' flux is flagged as scintillation, not phase, and that subaperture's contribution is down-weighted in that frame specifically (distinct from Problem 2/3's noise weighting, which is about steady low flux, not transient intensity dropouts).

---

## Problem 19 — MLA-to-detector registration drift
**Mechanism:** The mapping from "this pixel region belongs to this lenslet" is calibrated once. Thermal expansion of the MLA mount or detector housing, or mechanical settling, slowly shifts this registration. If undetected, spots drift toward the edge of their assigned subaperture box, biasing the "reference position" itself — a static offset error that looks exactly like a real wavefront tilt and gets baked into every Zernike measurement until manually recalibrated.

**Solution — Continuous reference-grid self-calibration:**
Track the long-term (minutes-to-hours timescale, distinct from frame-to-frame turbulence) mean position of each subaperture's spot. A slow, common-mode drift across many/all subapertures (rather than the fast, random, spatially-decorrelated motion characteristic of turbulence) indicates registration drift rather than atmosphere, and the reference grid is re-zeroed accordingly — separating "the MLA shifted" from "the sky tilted."

---

## Problem 20 — Loop timing jitter (not just constant lag)
**Mechanism:** Problem 8 addressed the ~1-2ms *average* latency, but real systems also have *jitter* — frame-to-frame variation in processing time (garbage collection, OS scheduling, variable network/bus transfer time for camera frames). A predictor tuned for a fixed 2ms delay degrades if the actual delay is sometimes 1ms and sometimes 4ms, because it's now predicting to the wrong point in time.

**Solution — Latency-aware prediction with timestamped frames:**
Hardware-timestamp every WFS frame and DM command at the point of capture/application (not at the point of software processing), and feed the *measured* per-frame latency into the predictor as an explicit input rather than assuming a fixed value — so Layer 2's predictor forecasts to the actual upcoming actuation time each frame, not a nominal average.

---

## Problem 21 — DM hysteresis and creep
**Mechanism:** Real DM actuators (especially piezo-based) don't move to exactly the commanded position — they exhibit hysteresis (the achieved position depends on recent command history, not just the current command) and slow creep after a large step. The conjugation step assumes "command X stroke → get X stroke," which is only approximately true and gets worse for fast, large, reversing commands — exactly the regime strong turbulence demands.

**Solution — Hysteresis-compensated command pre-shaping:**
Maintain a simple per-actuator hysteresis model (calibrated offline, e.g. a Preisach or rate-dependent model) and apply an inverse pre-shaping correction to the commanded stroke before sending it, so the actuator's *actual* achieved shape better matches the *intended* conjugate wavefront — folding naturally alongside the saturation-aware command generator (Problem 7) as part of the same final command-shaping stage.

---

## Problem 22 — Partial pupil illumination / vignetting at field edges or during partial cloud
**Mechanism:** The reconstruction matrix assumes a fixed, fully-illuminated pupil geometry. Vignetting at the field edge, partial cloud cover, or the telescope's secondary-mirror support structure can leave some subapertures partially or fully dark in a way that varies during an observation — those subapertures contribute biased or zero-flux measurements that, if reconstructed as if fully illuminated, distort the modal fit globally (because Zernike modes are fit to the full aperture, an error confined to a small dark region can leak into low-order modes affecting the whole image).

**Solution — Dynamic pupil masking:**
Continuously re-estimate which subapertures have usable flux above a minimum threshold and dynamically exclude the rest from the reconstruction fit, with the modal basis recomputed for the effective (possibly reduced) pupil shape each frame rather than always assuming the full nominal aperture — so a partly-clouded or vignetted frame degrades gracefully in the affected region instead of corrupting the global modal solution.

---

## Problem 23 — Differential tip-tilt / anisokinetism for guiding
**Mechanism:** Tip-tilt (overall image motion) measured by the WFS along the sensing line of sight isn't exactly the same as the tip-tilt affecting a point elsewhere in an extended solar field of view — similar in spirit to the isoplanatism problem (Problem 11) but specifically for the *lowest*-order mode, which dominates total image motion/smear and is usually treated as "solved" by guiding when it isn't fully valid off-axis.

**Solution — Ground/high-altitude separation applied specifically to tip-tilt:**
Apply the same ground-layer/high-altitude decomposition logic (Layer 3) at the tip-tilt level specifically, since tip-tilt error is often the single largest contributor to image degradation — ensuring the dominant mode, not just higher-order modes, benefits from the field-extension logic.

---

## Problem 24 — Daytime sky background raising the noise floor uniformly
**Mechanism:** Unlike night-time AO, a solar WFS operates against a bright scattered-sky background (especially at high airmass or with thin cirrus), which adds a roughly uniform background flux across all subapertures. This isn't classic photon noise on the signal (Problem 2) — it's a background pedestal that, if not subtracted correctly, biases every centroid slightly and shifts effective contrast, and it varies with time of day, airmass, and atmospheric clarity in a way that's specific to a solar telescope and basically irrelevant to night-time AO design literature.

**Solution — Real-time background pedestal subtraction:**
Continuously estimate the background level from regions of each subaperture frame outside the expected granulation signal footprint (or from periodic dark/sky frames), and subtract it before centroiding/CNN input — feeding the estimated background level itself as an auxiliary input to Layer 0's network so it can implicitly adjust its confidence under high-background conditions, tying into the same confidence framework as Problems 2 and 18.

---

## Problem 25 — Segment/edge actuator effects (if DM is segmented or has a stiff edge boundary)
**Mechanism:** Actuators near the DM's physical edge have fewer neighbours to share load with and different boundary conditions in their influence function (and if the DM is segmented, neighbouring segments may have phase discontinuities/piston error between them that a continuous-facesheet model doesn't represent). The saturation-redistribution logic (Problem 7) implicitly assumes a roughly uniform actuator neighbourhood, which breaks down exactly at the edges — often where the field-extension/ground-layer logic (Problem 11/12) needs the most help.

**Solution — Edge-aware redistribution weighting:**
Give the saturation-aware command generator (Layer 4) explicit knowledge of each actuator's boundary status (interior vs. edge vs. segment-boundary) so redistribution preferentially routes load to interior actuators with full neighbour support rather than treating all actuators as having equal redistribution capacity, and, if segmented, add a differential-piston monitor that tracks inter-segment phase jumps as a distinct error term outside the continuous Zernike basis.
